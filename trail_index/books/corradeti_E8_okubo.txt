                                                                             introduction and motivations
                                              Integral numbers in division algebras are a classical and fascinating topic at the interplay of
                                           algebra, arithmetic, and exceptional geometry. Already in the XIX century, Gauss [Gau] and
                                           Hurwitz [Hu19] extended the notion of integers to the complex numbers and the quaternions,
                                           identifying the Gaussian integers and the Hurwitz quaternions as natural integral structures.
                                           The culmination of this classical line of research is the Coxeter-Dickson octonions OE8 [Co46,
                                           Di23], the maximal order in the algebra of the octonions O, which is isometric to the exceptional
                                           lattice E8 . This octonionic background is also close to modern exceptional geometry and to the
                                           systematic study of octonionic Rosenfeld spaces [MCCAI23].
                                              The landscape of eight-dimensional real division algebras, however, extends well beyond the
                                           octonions. As a consequence of the Generalised Hurwitz Theorem [El18], among the eight-
                                           dimensional composition algebras over R there are three non-isomorphic division cases: the
                                           octonions O (unital), the para-octonions pO (para-unital), and the real Okubo algebra O (non-
                                           unital). While octonions have a unit element and para-octonions have a para-unit, the Okubo
                                           algebra merely contains idempotent elements, and its automorphism group is SU(3) rather than
                                           G2 . A synoptic comparison of these algebras is given in Table 3. This non-unital direction

has recently appeared in the study of Okubo projective geometry, Okubo Spin groups, minimal
Cayley-plane realisations, collineation groups of octonionic planes, and possible physical uses of
the Okubo algebra [CZ22, CMZ24, CMZ25, MCZ25].
   The question of integral elements for pO and O has, to our knowledge, not been systematically
separated from the classical unital case. The definition due to Dickson [Di23] and refined by
Johnson [Jo13, Jo17, Jo18] requires the presence of a unit element, and therefore does not directly
apply to non-unital algebras such as pO and O. In this work we isolate the part of Johnson’s
definition that survives for isotopes: closure under the relevant product, integrality of norm
and trace, and the relation with a discrete lattice or module. For complementary viewpoints
on eight-dimensional composition algebras, the Cayley plane, and their recovery from geometric
algebra, see also [Cor26, Cor24].
   The para-octonionic case gives a clean result. Since the Coxeter-Dickson order OE8 is closed
under octonionic conjugation and multiplication, it is also closed under x • y = x · y; hence the
same E8 lattice supports a Z-integral para-octonionic structure. This is a perfect analogy with
the octonionic case.
   The Okubo case is subtler and, to our knowledge, this is the first time that its integral
relation with the Coxeter-Dickson order is made completely explicit. The Petersson√product
x ∗ y = τ (x) · τ 2 (y) involves an automorphism whose matrix has coefficients in Q( 3), and
already on basic integral elements this introduces coefficients outside √ Z. The exact computation
shows that a diagonal 2-adic scaling produces an order over R = Z[ 3], but its direct rank-eight
shadow is not E8 . Rather, it is a conductor sublattice inside E8 of index 212 and determinant
224 .
   It is worth noting that this does not make the title literal in a naive sense. The Okubo integral
structure does not form the E8 lattice over Z. Instead, it gives rise to a 2-primary sublattice
from which E8 can be recovered by saturation or gluing. This distinction is the main arithmetic
point of the work.
   The paper is organised as follows. In Sec. 1 we review the classical notion of integral elements
over division algebras following Dickson and Johnson. In Sec. 2 we review the three algebras O,
pO, and O and√ their interplays. In Sec. 3 we prove the para-octonionic closure   √ theorem, exhibit
the Okubo 3-obstruction, and construct the scaled Okubo order over Z[ 3]. In Sec. 4 we
describe the Okubo-compatible conductor sublattice and recover E8 by saturation and gluing.
In Sec. 5 we replace continuous orbit claims by arithmetic stabilizers of the relevant order.
Finally, in Sec. 6 we discuss our findings and outline future directions.


                                    1. what is an integer
   Modern research on integral numbers started in the XIX century with Gauss, Kronecker
and Frege, culminating in the works of Dedekind, Peano [De88, Pe89] on the axiomatization of
natural and integer numbers. On the other hand, attempts to generalise the notion of integers
in the realm of division algebras such as C complex numbers and H quaternions were done
by Gauss[Gau], Hamilton and Hurwitz [Hu19]. From that chain of research the definition that
settled in the scientific community was that of Leonard Dickson in 1923 [Di23, p. 141-142] which
we briefly review here. Given a composition algebra A, i.e. an algebra with a norm n such that
n (x · y) = n (x) n (y), endowed with an involution x −→ x called conjugation, then a set I of
integral elements of A is a set that is closed under addition and multiplication that contains the
element 1 and such that for every element x ∈ I we have

(1.1)                                   tr (x) = x + x ∈ Z,
(1.2)                                      n (x) = xx ∈ Z.

Although this definition is broad and widely accepted, it has two notable shortcomings. First,
it can easily yield an infinite number of integral elements over Hurwitz division algebras, en-
compassing the real numbers R real numbers, the C complex numbers, the H quaternions

the O octonions. For example, for any natural number m ∈ N, the subring Z −m is a set
of integral elements of C. Second, its specificity -requiring the presence of the unit element-
automatically excludes integral systems from non-unital algebras, such as para-octonions pO
and Okubo algebra O.
   The first issue with Dickson’s definition was addressed by Johnson [Jo13, Jo17, Jo18]. Inspired
by the work of Coxeter [Co46], Johnson proposed that integral systems should inherently relate
to lattices and, by extension, to crystallographic root systems. He then arrived to the following
definition
Definition 1. A basic system of integral elements over a real algebra is given by a set of elements
such that:
    (1) The trace and the norm are integers. Specifically,
                                       tr (x) = x + x ∈ Z,

    (2) The elements form a subring of the algebra, closed under multiplication, and comprising
        a set of invertible unit elements.
    (3) The elements span a two-, four-, or eight-dimensional lattice embedded in R, C, H or O.

1.3. Octonionic integral sets. The octonionic case is considerably richer than its lower-

              2. Octonions, paraoctonions and the real Okubo algebra
   Lattices are often constructed by taking discrete subsets of unit norm elements within an
algebra and subsequently forming their integer span. Consequently, possessing a quadratic
norm that respects the multiplicative structure of the algebra results in an intriguing interplay
between the lattice elements and their algebraic properties. Normed algebras endowed with a
norm that respect the multiplicative structure, i.e., n (x · y) = n (x) n (y), are called composition
algebras.
   Composition algebras are divided in unital, i.e. where exists a unit element 1 such that
x · 1 = 1 · x = x, para-unital, i.e. where exists an involution x −→ x and an element called
paraunit 1 such that x · 1 = 1 · x = x, and, finally, non-unital, i.e. intended here for algebras
that possess neither a unit element nor a paraunit element.
   In fact, as a consequence of the Generalized Hurwitz Theorem [El18], over the real numbers,
the finite-dimensional composition algebras fall into exactly sixteen isomorphism classes: seven
are unital and called Hurwitz algebras, comprising the division algebras R, C, H, O along with
their split companions Cs , Hs , Os ; another seven are paraunital, closely related to the Hurwitz
algebras and termed para-Hurwitz algebras (see Table 2); finally, there are two composition

                       5. Arithmetic stabilizers and finite orbits
   In analogy with the octonionic case, it is natural to study the orbit structure of integral
elements. However, one must distinguish continuous automorphism groups from the finite arith-
metic groups that preserve a chosen order. The compact Lie group G2 acts on the real octonion
algebra, but it does not preserve the Coxeter-Dickson order in general. Likewise, the compact
group SU(3) acts on the real Okubo algebra, but it does not automatically preserve the scaled
order O0 or the conductor lattice LOk .

5.1. The octonionic and para-octonionic stabilizer. For the octonionic and para-octonionic
orders the relevant finite group is not the whole of G2 , but the arithmetic stabilizer
(5.1)                       Aut(O, OE8 ) = {g ∈ G2 : g(OE8 ) = OE8 }.
This is the group that can act on the 240 norm-one elements of the Coxeter-Dickson order. The
same stabilizer is relevant for the para-octonionic product, because the para product is defined
from octonionic conjugation and multiplication, both of which preserve the Coxeter-Dickson
order.
              INTEGRAL ELEMENTS OF OKUBO ALGEBRA AND THE E8-LATTICE                                    16


5.2. The Okubo arithmetic stabilizer. For the Okubo algebra, the corresponding object is
(5.2)                         Aut(O, O0 ) = {g ∈ SU(3) : g(O0 ) = O0 },
where O0 is the scaled R-order of Theorem 9. If the direct shadow is selected, one may further
restrict to
(5.3)                       Aut(O, LOk ) = {g ∈ SU(3) : g(LOk ) = LOk }.
Only these arithmetic stabilizers act on finite sets of integral elements. Consequently, any
statement about orbits of roots or integral elements must be formulated with respect to such a
discrete stabilizer, not with respect to the full compact continuous group SU(3).

                         6. Conclusions and Future Developments
   In this work we separated three layers that must not be conflated: the classical Euclidean
lattice E8 , the Coxeter-Dickson
                    √                octonionic order over Z, and the Okubo arithmetic order natu-
rally forced into Z[ 3]. The para-octonionic result is unconditional and mirrors the octonionic
case: the Coxeter-Dickson order is stable under x • y = x · y, and therefore gives a Z-integral
system for pO with underlying lattice E8 .
   For the Okubo algebra the situation is more mesmerizing and surprising. The        √ Petersson
product defined by the order-three automorphism τ introduces coefficients in Q( 3), and the
Coxeter-Dickson order is not closed as a Z-module.√ Nevertheless, after the diagonal scaling
D = diag(2, 2, 2, 2, 4, 4, 4, 4), one obtains a closed Z[ 3]-order O0 . Its direct metric shadow is
not E8 , but the conductor sublattice LOk of index 212 and determinant 224 inside E8 .
   The lattice E8 is then recovered in a precise but weaker sense: it is the 2-adic saturation of
LOk , equivalently the even unimodular overlattice obtained by gluing along H = E8 /LOk . This
recovery is metric-arithmetic rather than multiplicative, because the saturated lattice is again
not closed under the Okubo product. This is the exact sense in which the Okubo construction
is related to the E8 lattice.
   It would be definitely interesting to analyze whether larger multi-generator overorders of O0
give rise to finer conductor lattices, and whether the full arithmetic stabilizer of the Okubo
order contains nontrivial elements outside the signed block-permutation class tested here. In a
forthcoming work, it would also be natural to relate these conductor lattices to the polytope
structure of the Gosset polytope and to the arithmetic of the Okubo idempotent orbit.
